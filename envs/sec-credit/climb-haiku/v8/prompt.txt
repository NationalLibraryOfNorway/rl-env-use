When a question asks for the probability that a future reported figure (shares outstanding, cash balance, or similar) exceeds or falls below a threshold in the next filing period, calibrate as follows:

- For small-cap public companies that show ANY of: going-concern language, active ATM/ELOC/warrant programs, recent capital raises, net losses with limited cash runway, or stock-based compensation plans — the base probability that a share-count or cash threshold is crossed within a 3–6 month window is HIGH (0.75–0.90). These companies routinely issue shares or raise/deploy cash through mechanisms that are already in place.
- Only lower this probability substantially (below 0.5) if the materials contain strong, specific contrary evidence (e.g., the company has explicitly retired all equity programs, has large excess cash, and shows no dilution activity).
- CRITICAL: Do not let a large gap between the current figure and the threshold drive you below 0.75 when distress signals are present. A single capital raise, warrant exercise campaign, or ATM drawdown routinely issues millions of shares or raises millions of dollars in one quarter — far more than the steady-state quarterly run rate. The relevant question is not 'can the run rate close the gap linearly?' but 'could any plausible financing event close the gap?' If the answer is yes and distress signals are present, stay at 0.75–0.90.
- Worked example: company has 75M shares, threshold is 100M (gap = 25M), recent quarterly issuance rate is 4.5M shares, but company has going-concern doubt, active ELOC, and $2M cash. Linear projection yields only ~79M shares — far short. Yet a single equity raise or warrant exercise can easily issue 20–30M shares in one transaction. Assign ~0.80, not 0.20.
- Do not anchor solely on a linear extrapolation of the most recent quarter's issuance rate; small-cap companies often close large gaps in a single financing event when cash is tight.
