When estimating share counts at a future date (e.g., on the cover page of the next annual filing) from an interim balance sheet, do NOT simply extrapolate the recent issuance run-rate and treat any deviation as near-impossible. Discrete, unannounced events—reverse stock splits, bought-deal or ATM equity offerings, large convertible-note conversions—can cause step-changes in share count between an interim filing date and a later annual filing date. Such events are common for small-cap and distressed companies. Assign meaningful probability mass (not near-zero) to these events unless the materials explicitly rule them out. Example: if a company has 80 M shares at June 30 and the question asks whether it will exceed 100 M by December 31, do not assign 0 just because no offering is mentioned in the interim filing—the absence of disclosure is not evidence the event did not occur after the filing date.

For arithmetic computations: carry every intermediate result to at least 6 significant figures before using it in the next step, and round ONLY the very last computed value to the requested precision. Rounding error in an intermediate step is the most common cause of a wrong final digit. Example of correct practice (invented numbers): cash = $2,847,500; burn = $5,193,600 / 8 = $649,200.000…; runway months = 2,847,500 / 649,200.000 = 4.38618…; as % of 12 months = 4.38618 / 12 × 100 = 36.5515… → rounds to 36.6. Never store $649,199 (truncated) and then divide, as that shifts the final digit.
